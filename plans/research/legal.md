**Scope.** This is a research checklist for Darren Drake's firm to confirm. It is not legal advice. I researched it on 2026-09-24 and changed no repo files. tncourts.gov blocked automated access, so I read the Tennessee rule text from the Supreme Court's 2021 order and a copy of the current rules, both hosted on tbpr.org.

**Main finding.** Tennessee rewrote its lawyer-advertising rules effective **September 1, 2021** (Order ADM2020-01505):
- The old requirement to show the "name and office address" of a responsible lawyer (former RPC 7.2(d)) was deleted. It was replaced by **RPC 7.1(b): "name and contact information."**
- RPC 7.2, 7.4 and 7.5 are now "Deleted and Reserved." The rules on specialist claims and firm names moved into the comments to RPC 7.1.
- There is **no requirement to file ads with the Board**. There is a **2-year retention** requirement.
- Many third-party summaries (including the first search results) still quote the old "office address" wording.

Confidence levels: High = the rule text is clear and I read it; Medium = the application or current status is uncertain; Low = inference only.

## Part A: Verified (read in a primary source, or on an official .gov/.org page)

**A1. The website counts as a lawyer communication and advertising.**
- **Source:** Tenn. Sup. Ct. R. 8, RPC 7.1(a)-(b), comments [1], [7], [14]; RPC 7.3 comment [1].
  - Order: https://docs.tbpr.org/order-grants-tba-petition-to-amend-s-ct-rule-8-rpcs%207.1%207.2%207.3%207.4%207.5-denies-amendment%20to-s-ct-rule%208-rpc-7.6.pdf
  - Board's copy of current RPC 7 ("as amended effective September 1, 2021"): https://docs.tbpr.org/pub/current-tn-rpc7-justice-kirby-presentation-material.pdf
- **What it requires:**
  - Every communication about the lawyer's services must be truthful and not misleading.
  - A lawyer may advertise through "electronic communication, including public media."
  - Comment [14] lists "websites" among the types of advertisement.
  - RPC 7.3 comment [1] says a website or banner ad aimed at the general public "typically does not constitute a solicitation."
- **How it applies:** All page copy, meta descriptions, the Google Business Profile, social posts and paid ads fall under RPC 7.1. The site is not a solicitation, so Tennessee does not require an "Advertising Material" label on it.
- **Confidence:** High.

**A2. Name and contact information of a responsible lawyer.**
- **Source:** RPC 7.1(b), "shall include the name and contact information of at least one lawyer or law firm responsible for its content." Confirmed in the Board's newsletter (Board Notes, Fall 2021, Chief Disciplinary Counsel): https://docs.tbpr.org/pub/board-notes-fall-2021-2nd-issue.pdf
- **How it applies:**
  - Show "Darren Drake, Attorney at Law" and (615) 546-5551 on every page. The current footer already does this.
  - The Tennessee rule and comments do not define "contact information."
  - Add the office address once it is confirmed. It is also needed for marketing email (A14) and local search listings.
- **Confidence:** High that the requirement is name plus contact information. Medium on whether a phone number alone is enough.

**A3. Keep copies of ads; no filing with the Board.**
- **Source:** RPC 7.1(c) and comment [14]. Board FAQ (https://www.tbpr.org/for-legal-professionals/frequently-asked-questions): "Do I have to send copies of advertisements to the Board? Answer: No." The FAQ still cites the old rule numbers; the current ones are 7.1(c) and 7.3(e).
- **What it requires:** Keep a copy of each ad for 2 years after it was last shown, with a record of when and where it appeared. One copy covers ads that are the same in all material respects.
- **How it applies:**
  - Archive a dated snapshot (PDF or screenshots plus a git tag) of each materially changed version of the site, with a change log.
  - Do the same for paid ads, social posts and directory profiles.
  - Archive the current ddrakelaw.com before it is replaced. Its 2-year clock starts when it comes down.
- **Optional review:** The Board's Ethics Counsel takes informal inquiries, with "Advertising" as a category (Tenn. Sup. Ct. R. 9 §5.4; 1-800-486-5714): https://www.tbpr.org/for-legal-professionals/informal-ethics-inquiries. Opinion 2004-F-149 encourages submitting ads in advance.
- **Confidence:** High.

**A4. "Specialist" and certification claims.**
- **Source:** RPC 7.1 comments [9]-[10]. Tennessee CLE Commission page (https://cle.tncourts.gov/attorneys-main/attorneys-specialization/): "An attorney may advertise as a specialist only if they are certified by a national certification and registered in Tennessee," with an annual registration fee.
- **What it requires:**
  - The site may list practice areas, and may say he practices only in those fields if that is true.
  - It may not say "specialist," "specialty," "specializes in," or "certified/recognized as a specialist" unless (1) he is certified by an organization accredited by the ABA House of Delegates and (2) the certification is registered with the Tennessee CLE Commission.
  - It must not imply certification by the Tennessee Commission itself.
- **How it applies:** Use "practice focused on" or "practice areas," not "DUI specialist." Tennessee Attorney General Opinion 08-98 (2008) mentions an old Tennessee "DUI Defense Specialist" certification. That scheme is no longer how certification works; it now requires a national certifier.
- **Confidence:** High.

**A5. Misleading claims, comparisons, past results and awards.**
- **Source:** RPC 7.1(a) and comments [2], [3], [4], [8].
- **What it requires:**
  - A truthful statement is still misleading if it leads a reasonable person to a conclusion with no factual basis.
  - Reported results can create "unjustified expectation[s]."
  - Comparisons with other lawyers that cannot be backed up are misleading.
  - A disclaimer "may preclude" a finding that something is misleading. It is not a safe harbor.
  - Awards are allowed only if the awarding organization looked into the lawyer's fitness and does not hand them out indiscriminately or for a price.
  - RPC 8.4(e) prohibits implying an ability to improperly influence officials.
- **How it applies:** No "we win" statements, results counters, "best" claims, or anything implying influence with prosecutors or courts. Check each award's criteria before listing it.
- **Confidence:** High.
- **Stricter Board guidance:** Formal Ethics Opinion 2004-F-149 (https://www.tbpr.org/ethic_opinions/2004-f-149) says:
  - no past results unless they describe the specific facts and legal circumstances;
  - testimonials from simulated clients need a prominent notice;
  - paid testimonials are prohibited;
  - self-descriptions like "best," "top," "experienced," "highly qualified" or "reputable" are not allowed without factual substantiation.
- **Status of that opinion:** It is still listed and not vacated on the Board's index. But it interprets the pre-2021 rule text, so treat it as conservative guidance. Confidence in its current weight: Medium.

**A6. Testimonials and reviews.**
- **Source:** RPC 7.3(f) and comment [10]: nothing of value may be given for "recommending" a lawyer, which includes endorsing or vouching for him. FTC rule 16 CFR Part 465 (eCFR current as of 2026-09-22):
  - §465.2 bans fake reviews and testimonials;
  - §465.4 bans incentives tied to a positive or negative review;
  - §465.5 requires disclosure on reviews by insiders;
  - §465.7 bans suppressing reviews with groundless threats.
- **Confidentiality:** RPC 1.6(a) bars revealing "information relating to the representation" without the client's informed consent. The Board quotes this in Opinion 2015-F-159.
- **How it applies:**
  - Keep the site free of reviews and testimonials until the firm has a written policy.
  - No gift cards or discounts for reviews.
  - Get written informed consent before using any testimonial or case result.
  - Responses to negative Google reviews must not reveal client information.
- **Confidence:** High on the rules. Medium on how they apply to review responses.

**A7. Solicitation limits (intake form, chat, texting, calls).**
- **Source:** RPC 7.3(a)-(e) and comments [1], [2], [8], [9].
- **What it defines and requires:**
  - A solicitation is a targeted communication to a specific person about a particular matter.
  - Websites, search-triggered ads and responses to someone's request for information are not solicitations.
  - Soliciting by live person-to-person contact for pecuniary gain is prohibited. That means in person, live phone, or real-time video or voice. The rule says it "does not include chat rooms, text messages, email."
  - Written or electronic solicitations to a person known to need legal services in a particular matter must:
    - carry the words "Advertising Material" at the beginning and end;
    - not show the subject matter on the outside of an envelope;
    - be kept for 2 years with a record of recipients;
    - stop if the person asks, with no coercion, harassment or overreaching.
  - The 30-day rule in 7.3(d)(3) lists personal injury, divorce, workers' compensation, wrongful death, accidents and disasters. Criminal charges are not listed.
- **How it applies:**
  - The intake form, click-to-call, and callbacks to people who reached out first are fine.
  - Staff must not cold-call people, for example from arrest or jail lists.
  - Mail, email or texts to arrestees trigger the "Advertising Material" label, record-keeping and opt-out rules.
  - A text-based chat widget is not "live" contact, but RPC 7.1 and confidentiality still apply.
- **Confidence:** High.

**A8. Marketing vendors, lead generators and referral services.**
- **Source:**
  - RPC 7.3(f)(1) and comment [10].
  - RPC 7.6, amended effective January 1, 2022; Rule 44 registration was deleted (https://www.tbpr.org/for-legal-professionals/intermediary-organizations).
  - Opinions 2018-F-165 (https://www.tbpr.org/ethic_opinions/2018-f-165-legal-services-websites) and 2006-F-152 (https://www.tbpr.org/ethic_opinions/2006-f-152).
- **What it requires:**
  - The firm may pay website designers and marketers, as long as they do not direct the lawyer's judgment.
  - It may pay lead generators only if they do not recommend the lawyer, do not imply the referral is unpaid, and do not analyze the person's legal problem.
  - No fee sharing with nonlawyers.
  - For referral services and similar organizations, the lawyer must make reasonable efforts to ensure their conduct complies (RPC 7.6(b)).
- **How it applies:** Vet the copy of any DUI lead service or directory before paying for it.
- **Confidence:** High.

**A9. Firm name and trade names.**
- **Source:** RPC 7.1 comments [11]-[13].
- **What it says:**
  - Trade names are allowed if not misleading. One that includes a place name may need a disclaimer that it is not a public legal aid agency.
  - Using the name of a lawyer not associated with the firm is misleading.
  - Lawyers who only share an office may not present themselves as a firm.
- **How it applies:**
  - Keep "Darren Drake, Attorney at Law."
  - Do not show another firm's name or branding.
  - If he practices alone, avoid "our attorneys" or "our team of lawyers" (this last point is an inference from 7.1(a)).
- **Confidence:** High on the rule. Medium on the wording point.

**A10. Keeping intake data confidential.**
- **Sources:**
  - Opinion 2015-F-159 on cloud storage (https://www.tbpr.org/ethic_opinions/2015-f-159). It quotes RPC 1.6 comments [15]-[16]: take reasonable precautions when transmitting client information, and "special circumstances... may warrant special precautions."
    - Pick providers with reasonable care, with a confidentiality agreement.
    - Know where the data is stored and how to get it back if the service ends.
    - Keep backups and train staff on passwords.
    - Supervise vendors under RPC 5.3.
  - A 2021 CLE paper hosted by the Board (https://docs.tbpr.org/pub/dean-koch-presentation-materials.pdf) quotes RPC 1.6: "make reasonable efforts to prevent the inadvertent or unauthorized disclosure of, or unauthorized access to" client information, with the reasonableness factors in comment 18.
- **How it applies:**
  - Send intake over HTTPS only.
  - Deliver inquiries to a system with confidentiality terms, multi-factor login, and access limited to named people.
  - Keep inquiry content out of analytics, logs and error trackers. The current analytics stub already records event names only.
  - Hosting providers and developers count as nonlawyer assistants. Sign confidentiality or data-processing terms with them and plan how to export the data.
- **Confidence:** High on the duty. Medium on specific controls; the Board declines to set a standard of care.

**A11. Tennessee Information Protection Act (state privacy law).**
- **Source:** Tennessee Attorney General release, April 30, 2025: https://www.tn.gov/attorneygeneral/news/2025/4/30/pr25-25.html
- **What it says:**
  - Effective July 1, 2025.
  - Applies only to businesses with more than $25M in revenue **and** either (a) personal information of 175,000 or more Tennessee consumers a year, or (b) 25,000 or more consumers plus over 50% of revenue from selling personal information.
  - Nonprofits and certain other entities are exempt.
  - Covered businesses must publish a privacy notice covering: categories of data, purposes, how to exercise rights, appeals, what is shared and with whom.
  - Also: opt-out of targeted advertising, consent for sensitive data, a 60-day right to cure, and a defense for following the NIST privacy framework.
- **How it applies:** A small firm is almost certainly not covered. Still use the Act's notice list as the template for a voluntary privacy notice; the current /privacy page is a placeholder.
- **Confidence:** High on the thresholds. The firm should confirm its revenue.

**A12. Federal texting and robocall rules (only if the firm starts texting).**
- **Source:** 47 CFR 64.1200(a)(1)-(2), (a)(10) and (f) (eCFR current as of 2026-09-22).
- **What it requires:**
  - Autodialed or prerecorded calls and texts to cell phones need prior express consent.
  - Marketing messages need prior express *written* consent. It must disclose that consent is not a condition of purchase.
  - People can revoke consent by any reasonable means ("stop," "quit," and so on). The firm must honor it within 10 business days at most and cannot require one specific method.
- **How it applies:** Leave texting out, as the brand guidelines already say. If it is added, use a separate, optional, unchecked box that is not required to submit intake, and keep a record of consent.
- **Confidence:** High on the rule. Medium on whether a given texting platform counts as an autodialer.

**A13. Marketing email (CAN-SPAM).**
- **Source:** FTC compliance guide: https://www.ftc.gov/business-guidance/resources/can-spam-act-compliance-guide-business
- **What it requires:** Accurate headers, a truthful subject line, identification as an ad, a valid physical postal address, and a working opt-out honored within 10 business days. Transactional and relationship messages are mostly exempt.
- **How it applies:** Replying to an inquiry is fine. Newsletters need the postal address.
- **Confidence:** High.

**A14. Accessibility law.**
- **Sources:**
  - 42 U.S.C. §12181(7)(F) lists the "office of an accountant or lawyer" as a public accommodation (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/12181).
  - Justice Department guidance, March 18, 2022 (https://www.ada.gov/resources/web-guidance/): the ADA applies to services "offered on the web." WCAG and Section 508 are "helpful guidance." Businesses have "flexibility" in how they comply. The guidance is not binding. It lists common barriers: contrast, color alone, alt text, captions, form labels and errors, keyboard access.
  - The 2024 Justice Department rule for state and local governments (https://www.ada.gov/resources/2024-03-08-web-rule/) adopts WCAG 2.1 AA. It does not apply to private firms. An interim rule on April 20, 2026 pushed its deadlines back one year.
  - WCAG 2.2 is a W3C Recommendation, current version dated December 12, 2024 (https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/): "Content that conforms to WCAG 2.2 also conforms to WCAG 2.0 and WCAG 2.1."
  - Litigation volume (Seyfarth, a law-firm blog, not a primary source): 3,117 federal website-accessibility suits in 2025, up 27% from 2,452 (https://www.adatitleiii.com/2026/03/federal-court-website-accessibility-lawsuit-filings-bounce-back-in-2025/).
  - FTC final order against accessiBe, April 22, 2025: $1M for claiming its AI widget makes sites WCAG-compliant (https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/04/ftc-approves-final-order-requiring-accessibe-pay-1-million).
- **Why WCAG 2.2 AA is a reasonable target:** it includes WCAG 2.1 AA, the standard the government adopted for itself.
- **How it applies:**
  - Don't use accessibility overlay widgets.
  - Don't claim "ADA compliant."
  - Publish an accessibility statement with a phone number and email for help.
  - Keep the axe tests and add manual keyboard and screen-reader checks.
- **Confidence:** High on the sources. Medium on how much lawsuit risk this particular firm faces.

## Part B: Unverified (secondary sources, or no primary source found)

- **U1. Rule changes since 2021.** I could not load the current Rule 8 text on tncourts.gov, and searches found no changes to RPC 7.1 or 7.3 after 2021. Confirm there before launch.
- **U2. What "contact information" means.** The ABA Model Rule comment says a phone number, email, website or office address each count. That is persuasive only, and I could not read it (the ABA site blocked access).
- **U3. Board website-retention guidelines.** Comment [14] says the Board "may adopt" guidelines for websites. I found none.
- **U4. Prospective-client rule (RPC 1.18) in Tennessee.**
  - A 2013 Tennessee Alliance for Legal Services training quotes comment [2]: someone who sends information "unilaterally... without any reasonable expectation" that the lawyer will discuss representation is not a prospective client.
  - I could not confirm whether Tennessee adopted the ABA's 2012 website wording, which calls for "clear and reasonably understandable warnings." Keep the disclaimer next to the form (already done); an acknowledgement checkbox is optional.
  - Collect only what a conflict check needs. Ask people not to describe the alleged offense.
  - RPC 1.18(c): "significantly harmful" information from someone who inquires can bar the firm from representing someone adverse to them, such as a co-defendant. Medium.
- **U5. Data-breach notice law, Tenn. Code §47-18-2107** (per a Davis Wright Tremaine summary). It covers a name plus a Social Security number, driver's license number, or financial account number; notice is due within 45 days; encrypted data is exempt. Do not collect Social Security or license numbers in web intake. Medium.
- **U6. Cookie consent.** I found no general Tennessee or federal cookie-consent law. A banner is likely unnecessary with cookieless analytics and no ad pixels. Medium-Low.
- **U7. Retargeting ads.** I found no Tennessee authority on whether retargeting is solicitation. Comment [1] treats banner and search-generated ads as advertising, so retargeting is probably the same. Separately, ad pixels can send page URLs or form data to ad platforms, which is a confidentiality risk. No pixels on /intake. Medium.
- **U8. Sixth Circuit (covers Tennessee).** Secondary sources cite *Parker v. MetLife*, 121 F.3d 1006 (6th Cir. 1997), as limiting "public accommodation" to physical places. A law office is a physical place, so the risk remains. Low-Medium.
- **U9. AI chatbots.** Verified: the Board's formal-opinion index has no AI or chatbot opinion (the latest are 2025-F-171 and 2025-F-172, the latter withdrawn). ABA Formal Opinion 512 is cited by secondary sources only. If a chatbot is used, RPC 7.1, 1.6, 1.18 and 5.3 would apply.
- **U10. DUI after a crash.** The 7.3(d)(3) text covering matters that "relate to an accident" is verified. Whether it reaches a DUI arising from a crash is untested. Ask the Board's Ethics Counsel before any targeted mail or texts. Low.
- **U11. Client consent for public-record results.** ABA Formal Opinion 480 says consent is needed even for public-record case results. Persuasive only, not read.
- **U12. Edited portrait.** I found no Tennessee authority on retouching. It is a genuine likeness with the background replaced. Get Darren's written approval and confirm the photographer's usage rights. Low risk.
- **U13. Not researched:** the Tennessee Consumer Protection Act as applied to lawyer ads, Tennessee wiretap law as applied to session replay (keep it off), and Opinion 2023-F-170 on credit-card payments (only relevant if the site takes payments).

## Questions for the firm

1. What office address should be published, and is it the one registered with the Board? Is Darren the "responsible lawyer"? What email should be listed?
2. Is Darren a solo practitioner? What is the legal entity name, and is there any trade name?
3. Does he hold any board certification from an ABA-accredited organization, and is it registered with the Tennessee CLE Commission? If not, confirm there will be no "specialist" wording.
4. Which bar admissions, years, Board registration number and federal court admissions should be listed? Please confirm the Navy service and community-involvement wording.
5. Will the site ever show testimonials, reviews, case results or awards? If so, who approves them, and what is the client-consent process?
6. Where will intake go (email, Clio or another CRM)? Who has access? Is multi-factor login on? What is the retention period for message content versus conflict-check data, and who handles deletion? Are vendor confidentiality agreements in place?
7. Will the firm use live chat, an AI chatbot, texting, newsletters, retargeting pixels, paid lead services or directories?
8. Does the firm, or will it, send mail, email or texts to arrestees?
9. Which analytics tool will be used? Is it cookieless? Who owns the privacy notice?
10. Who will keep 2-year archives of each site version and each ad? Should ddrakelaw.com be archived before it is replaced?
11. Will fees be published (flat fees, payment plans, card payments)?
12. Does Darren approve the edited portrait for publication, and does the firm hold the photographer's usage rights?
13. Who is the contact for accessibility requests on the accessibility statement?
14. Can you confirm annual revenue is under $25M (so the Tennessee privacy act does not apply)?
15. Should the final site go to the Board's Ethics Counsel for informal review before launch?
